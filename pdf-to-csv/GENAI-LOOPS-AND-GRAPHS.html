Loops and graphs v0.1 · a reading of the process, 2026-10-23 · no real data on this page

Shop load loops

The software we built is a straight line: pulls in, estimates and maps out. Every loop in the process runs through people and the yard, and comes back on the next pull. The graphs are what the line carries. This page names each loop by how it closes, draws the graphs, and marks the three loops that do not close yet.

How a loop closes: by itself the tools do it on the next draw or pull through a person someone has to act for it to come round inside the code an iteration with a stop condition open nothing closes it today

Everything here is an Assessment of the design, except where a sentence says "built", which is a fact about the tested prompts. Companion: the flow page.

Part 1 · the loops

Six loops, from the slowest to the fastest

Solid pills are steps the tools take. Dashed pills are steps a person takes. The dashed label at the end says what carries the loop back to its start.

1. The history loop

through a person one turn = one availability · learning loop · the one that teaches the estimator
JCN arrives→ estimator scores it against history→ consumption.csv→ map shows the load→ planners plan it→ planned rows replace the guess→ the work runs→ hours charged, material ordered→ next reporting-tool pull→ history is one job richer · back to the start

Reinforcing: every completed job makes the next analog search better, because the history is the estimator's only memory. Its weak spots are the two open loops below (nothing compares the estimate with what was charged, and deficiency hours never come back) and grouped JCNs, which the division by the JCN count handles.

2. The planning loop

by itself one turn = one pull · balancing · built (Prompts D)
pull carries planning status→ estimator skips planned summaries→ guessed share of the map falls→ next pull, more planned→ until nothing is guessed

Balancing: as the planning floor catches up with the work package, the guessed share goes to zero on its own. The map is a mix of planner numbers and history numbers, and the mix shifts toward the planners as the availability nears.

3. The what-if loop

through a person one turn = minutes, then one pull · the leveler, built (series B2)
look at the map→ move a job, slip a project, farm out→ see the effect at once→ keep or undo→ export moves.csv→ someone keys the moves into the schedule of record→ next pull shows them as dates of record→ the overlay is empty again

The first half runs in seconds and is built. The second half is a person with the moves list. If nobody keys the moves in, the what-if and the record drift apart, and the next pull quietly puts everything back where it was.

4. The capacity loop

through a person one turn = one pull · balancing against the 80 to 100 percent band
a shop-week over the band→ farm out, lend people, overtime→ the workforce map changes→ next pull→ the cell is back in the band

The map is the gauge and the band is the setpoint. The contractor line is where farmed work lands, so the gauge also shows whether the contractor is the one going over.

5. The leveler's own loops

inside the code milliseconds · built (B2-4) · stops on its own
for each movable job, by least float→ try every shift inside the window→ keep the smallest peak→ next job
find the worst shop-week over the band→ send one job to the contractor line→ repeat until nothing is over, a week has no candidate (overtime), or 10,000 passes

Each pass removes one overload and never adds one back, so the loop terminates. Its answers are suggestions until Apply, when they join loop 3 as moves marked auto.

6. The build loop

through a person hours · how the tools themselves are made
write the prompt→ the model writes the module→ assemble, test against an independent twin→ fix the prompt text, never the code→ regenerate→ pull request, review, ruling→ prompt version n+1

The fix always goes into the prompt, so the thing that improves is the thing that gets reused inside the enclave. Two outer turns sit on it: the reviewer's points and your rulings, one of which reversed an inferred rule last night.

Part 2 · the graphs

What the line carries

Five drawings. Boxes are nodes, lines are edges, and the labels on edges are what the tools actually store on them.

A. The flow is a DAG; the cycle runs outside it

Inside the files nothing feeds back: pulls, estimate, map, outputs, in that order. The only cycles are the ones in Part 1, and each of them passes through the dashed edge at the bottom.

B. The work is a tree with one many-to-one

Everything hangs off the job summary in one-to-many steps, except JCN to job summary, which is many-to-one when jobs were grouped. That one edge is why hours are shared out over the JCNs and the material list is given whole with a group size.

C. The load is a bipartite graph: work on one side, shop-weeks on the other

A map cell is the sum of the edges into it; the drill-down walks the edges back to their rows. A job move slides all of a job's edges along the week axis together. A farm-out re-points them from the organic shop to the contractor node. The leveler is a search over where the edges land, one job at a time, keeping each job's phases in step.

D. The estimate is a nearest-neighbor graph

Each new JCN gets a weighted edge to every past JCN in its class, and the few heaviest edges supply the phases, the hours and the material. Changing a weight in the panel reshapes this graph for every item at once.

E. The file is a chain of wrappers

Order is the whole content of this graph. Every ordering fault the tests found was an edge pointing the wrong way in this chain, which is why the build guide fixes the sequence and why a hook prompt names what it captures.

F. Two small matching graphs, and one path

The lookup joins the workforce map's project names to the estimator's project ids, one edge per project (Prompt L drafts it, you confirm each edge). The shop codes are matched across sheets the same way ("Shop 11" and "11" are one node). Inside a job, the phases are a path, one after another; between jobs there are no edges at all, which is the leveler's known simplification: it treats jobs as independent, so a tie like "job B after job A" is not honored.

Part 3 · the loops that do not close

Three gaps, each with what would close it

7

Calibration: estimate against actual open

The estimator guesses from history and never hears how the guess did. The data to close it is already in the pulls: est_hours and charged_hours per phase, and the consumption lines the estimator wrote for jobs that have since run.

Closes it: one report per shop and phase title, estimate low to high against charged, from the last pull; then a nudge to the weights, by hand at first. A small prompt on the estimator, not yet written.

8

Deficiency and emergent hours open

Work the package did not predict is charged somewhere, but it never feeds the estimate of the next package, which is the reason history understates effort. The emergent reserve was deferred on 2026-10-22.

Closes it: the reserve per shop and fifth of the availability from JCNs received after the start, as the first design had it, once real pulls carry received dates and project ids.

9

Were the moves absorbed? open

Loop 3 closes only if someone keys the moves list into the schedule of record. Nothing checks, on the next pull, whether the dates of record moved the way the overlay said they should.

Closes it: on import, compare the stored moves with the new baseline (did P-003 really start two weeks later?), mark each move absorbed or still pending, and clear the absorbed ones. A one-prompt addition to series B2.
